In thirteen eighty-six, a pig in France was put on trial for murder. And it lost.

Picture the town of Falaise, in Normandy. A sow had attacked a little child. The child was badly hurt, and later died.

So the court did what courts did back then. It put the animal on trial, the way it would a person.

The sentence was brutal. The sow was to be mangled and maimed about the head and forelegs, then hanged.

The town came to watch. Crowds gathered in the square.

Here's the strange part. Animals really were treated as legal creatures in medieval courts. Judges thought a crime needed a punishment, even when the criminal walked on four legs.

You see, the trial was never really about the pig. The village had lost a child, and people needed something to answer for it.

Hanging the animal gave the town a sense that the wrong had been put right.

The hangman got paid, too. His receipt still survives, and it even mentions a new glove for the job.

So the pig lost. It was the only one in that courtroom who never got to say a word.
