In eighteen-oh-six, a Boston newspaper printed a story that began with two words. No joke.

A young man named Frederic Tudor had filled a ship with ice. Real ice, cut from frozen ponds. And he was sending it to Martinique, a hot island in the Caribbean.

The paper hoped it would not turn out to be a slippery speculation.

It did. The ice arrived, but there was nowhere to keep it, and nobody knew what to do with it. It melted away. Tudor lost about four thousand dollars.

He tried again, and lost again. By eighteen-thirteen, he had been locked in jail for his debts.

You see, the problem was never the ice. People in hot places had never had a cold drink. You can't miss something you've never tasted.

So Tudor stopped selling ice, and started selling cold. He built icehouses in hot ports. He packed his ice in sawdust, free trash from lumber mills. And he gave bartenders ice for free, until their customers got hooked on cold drinks.

In eighteen thirty-three, he sent a hundred and eighty tons of ice on a four-month trip to India. About a hundred tons made it.

He died rich. People called him the Ice King.

So that newspaper was right all along. It really was no joke.
